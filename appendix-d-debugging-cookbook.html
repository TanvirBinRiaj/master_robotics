Debugging Cookbook

Your robot is dead and the clock is ticking. Find your symptom below, check the likely causes in order, and run the test that proves each one.

Reference All levels Appendix D of D
Safety first

Smell burning or see swelling batteries? Stop. Cut power, move the battery to a safe spot, and follow Chapter 3. No debug is worth a fire.

The golden rule: halves, not guesses

Do not change three things at once. Split the system in half: is power fine? Then power is innocent. Is the brain alive? Then the brain is innocent. Each test should clear half the suspects. Five good tests beat fifty random tweaks.

Debug order Power → brain → signals → software → mechanics

Follow this order because each layer feeds the next. A dead battery looks exactly like broken code.

Dead on arrival: nothing lights up

  1. Measure the battery with a multimeter. Compare with the label in Chapter 12.
  2. Check polarity: plus to plus, minus to minus. Reverse polarity kills boards fast.
  3. Check the switch, fuse and e-stop chain. Wiggle each while watching the meter.
  4. Measure voltage at the board input while the switch is on. A big drop means a bad wire or connector.

Brownout: brain resets when motors move

The classic symptom: the robot boots, the wheels spin, the brain reboots. The motors steal current and the voltage sags below the brain's minimum.

SymptomLikely causeTest
Reset exactly when motors startWeak battery or thin wiresMeter on brain supply; watch the dip during a stall
Reset only at full speedStall current above supply limitLimit PWM to 50%; if resets stop, size power per Ch. 6
Random resets near radiosShared-impedance noise (EMI)Separate motor and logic grounds at one star point

Fixes that work: a battery with higher discharge rating, thicker motor wires, a separate regulator for logic, and bulk capacitors at the driver input.

Motors hum but do not move

  1. Check the driver enable pin and mode jumpers (see Chapter 6).
  2. Confirm the PWM signal reaches the driver: meter or scope on the input pin.
  3. Check stall: can you turn the shaft by hand with power off? A jammed gearbox hums exactly like this.
  4. For steppers: verify coil pairs with a meter. Swapped coils vibrate instead of turning.

Motor driver faults (CiA 402 / EtherCAT)

Industrial drives report fault codes, not silence. Read them first. Common ones: over-current (too much load or too fast a ramp), over-voltage (braking energy with nowhere to go), following error (encoder says the motor lags the command).

Note

Vendor quirks are real: some drives need a homing run (mode 6) before cyclic modes 8/9/10 work, and some ignore commands until the state machine reaches Operation Enabled. See Chapter 27.

Bad joints and cold solder

A cold joint looks dull, blobby or cracked. It works when you press it and fails when you let go. Reheat with flux until it flows shiny, as in Chapter 13. For connectors: tug-test every crimp. A wire that slides out with a gentle pull was never connected.

Sensor lies: noisy or frozen readings

  1. Print raw values with nothing moving. Frozen numbers mean wiring or address trouble (wrong I2C address is the top suspect).
  2. Wave your hand: do numbers move at all? Movement with wrong scale means a calibration error (Chapter 10).
  3. Noise that grows when motors run means EMI: twist motor wires, route signal wires away, add the scope check below.

Scope in five minutes

You do not need to master the oscilloscope. Three checks solve most cases: supply rail during a motor start (dip or spike?), PWM pin (clean square wave at the right rate?), encoder lines (both channels toggling while you turn the shaft by hand?). If the rail dips, fix power. If PWM is wrong, fix code. If encoders are dead, fix wiring.

ROS 2: no data on the topic

  1. Run ros2 node list and ros2 topic list. Is your node and topic even there?
  2. Run ros2 topic echo /your_topic. Data here but not in your code means a message-type or QoS mismatch.
  3. Check DDS connectivity across machines: same domain ID, working multicast. Logs in Chapter 27 show the exact knobs.

Reading datasheets and errata

When nothing makes sense, the vendor already warned you. Search the datasheet for "errata", "absolute maximum" and "typical application". Check: supply limits, pin modes after reset, and timing diagrams. One errata line ("ADC reads high when motor PWM exceeds 20 kHz") can save a week.

Tip

Write down the exact part number and revision before searching. Errata attaches to revisions, not part families. Keep a project log of every quirk you find; it becomes your team's private database.

Key takeaways

  • Debug in halves: power, brain, signals, code.
  • Brownouts look like software bugs; measure the rail first.
  • Drive fault codes and datasheet errata tell you the answer.
  • Log every quirk; it becomes your errata database.
