Chapter 26: Whole-Body Control and MPC

You will command many joints as one body, stack tasks by rank, and run MPC inside tight time budgets.

~30 min read Advanced Chapter 26 of 30

What you will learn

1. Why PID alone is not enough

Picture a humanoid carrying a tray of drinks. Its feet must stay planted firmly. Its hands must keep the tray level. Its knees must bend without falling over. One joint cannot solve this alone.

A plain PID drives each joint to its own target. It worked well in control basics. But it sees no whole-body balance. It also fights contact forces blindly. The robot wobbles and drops the tray.

Inverse dynamics fixes this with a body model. It asks what forces create a wanted motion. It then commands those torques at once. Think of a crew rowing together, not six solo boats.

From joints to jobs

Task-space control commands the job, not joints. You say "keep the hand here". The math picks all joint moves. You stop hand-tuning twelve loops by feel.

This builds on kinematics and Chapter 25 math. Poses come from SE(3). Best moves come from QP solvers. Now we stack many jobs at once.

In plain English

A task is one job for the body, like "hold balance". The nullspace is the free wiggle room left after a job is done. Lower jobs may only use that free room.

2. Task space and the nullspace idea

Think of writing on a whiteboard. Your hand must follow the letters. That is the main task. Your elbow can still move many ways. Those extra options form the nullspace.

The robot uses the Jacobian as a translator. It turns joint speeds into hand speeds. It also turns hand forces into joint torques. One matrix links both directions at once.

Control in task space has two clear steps. First, fix the main job exactly. Second, spend leftover freedom on comfort jobs. Comfort means bent knees, low effort, or looking ahead.

A two-job pattern you can reuse

  1. Solve the top job: balance the body over the feet.
  2. Freeze its result, then solve the next job inside leftovers.
  3. Add gentle posture wishes last, with small weights.
  4. Send the combined torques to all joints together.
Task map hand_speed = J × joint_speed
Nullspace filter safe_move = (I − J⋅ J) × comfort_wish
Note

J⋅ means a safe pseudo-inverse of J. It picks the smallest joint move for a task. The filter then removes any part that would break the main job.

3. Hierarchy: strict ranks versus soft wishes

Some tasks must never lose. Balance beats waving hello. Feet contact beats pretty posture. These are strict ranks. A lower task cannot touch them at all.

Other tasks are soft wishes with weights. Gaze at a face scores 10 points. Save energy scores 2 points. The solver trades them off smoothly. No wish can break a strict rank.

Regularization adds a tiny calm-down cost. It tells the solver to prefer small, smooth answers. Think of adding a pinch of salt. Too little does nothing, too much ruins taste.

4. Worked example: reach while balancing

A humanoid must reach a shelf and stay upright. The shelf target is 10 cm forward. The feet must not slide at all. We solve this in strict order.

Step 1: lock the feet

Rank one says foot speed equals zero. It also caps push forces inside friction cones. Suppose each foot may push up to 300 N. The solver keeps forces inside this safe cone.

Step 2: move the hand in leftovers

Rank two asks for 10 cm forward hand motion. The nullspace filter removes foot-breaking parts. Say the raw wish needs knee twist 5°. The filter trims it to 1°. Balance survives the reach.

Step 3: spend the rest on comfort

  1. Keep knees slightly bent for shock safety.
  2. Point the head toward the shelf target.
  3. Minimize total torque with a small weight.

This pattern is called HQP, or hierarchical QP. Each rank is one QP puzzle. Lower ranks inherit equality locks from above. Research teams report a 1.2 m/s walk with an 800 Hz QPOASES loop and a 100 Hz planner, as one lab example.

Tip

Test hierarchy with a freeze check. Command the hand while watching foot force. If feet move, your rank lock leaks. Fix locks before tuning weights.

5. One QP for the whole body

Whole-body control solves joints and contacts together. The variable is ξ = [accelerations, contact forces]. Accelerations move the body mass. Forces push against the ground safely.

Equality rows enforce physics and locked tasks. Inequality rows enforce friction, torque caps, and joint stops. The cost blends task errors plus small regularization. One solve returns all commands at once.

Why this beats separate loops

Separate loops fight over shared joints every tick. One QP sees all fights at once. It picks a trade both loops accept. Contacts stay legal by construction, not luck.

Python 
import numpy as np

# Task weights: balance is strict (locked), others are soft wishes.
W_balance = 1000.0
W_hand = 50.0
W_posture = 1.0
W_reg = 0.01

def qp_cost(xi, err_balance, err_hand, err_posture):
    return (W_balance * err_balance**2
            + W_hand * err_hand**2
            + W_posture * err_posture**2
            + W_reg * float(xi @ xi))

def mpc_loop(steps=5, dt=0.01):
    x = measure_state()          # sensors -> body state
    x_warm = last_solution()     # warm-start from prior tick
    for k in range(steps):       # short horizon plan
        xi = solve_one_qp(x, x_warm)  # accels + forces, Ax <= b
        x = predict(x, xi, dt)   # model rolls one step ahead
        x_warm = xi              # next tick starts nearby
    cmd = first_step_only()      # play one frame, then replan
    send_torques(cmd)
    return cmd

6. The MPC loop and real-time budgets

MPC plans a short movie and plays one frame. Then it measures again and replans. This loop loves fresh data. It forgives bumps, slips, and pushes well.

Real robots run two clocks at once. A fast whole-body QP runs at 800 Hz to 1 kHz. A slower planner runs near 100 Hz over seconds. The fast loop tracks, the slow loop dreams ahead.

Warm-start reuses the last answer as a guess. Most ticks change only a little. The solver then finishes in far fewer steps. Cascaded-fidelity MPC uses a rough far future and a sharp near future.

A budget you can copy

  1. Measure and estimate state: under 0.2 ms.
  2. Solve the whole-body QP: under 0.8 ms at 1 kHz.
  3. Update the slow SQP plan in the background at 100 Hz.
  4. If a tick overruns, replay a safe fallback torque.

A kino-dynamics planner mixes motion shape with force needs. It sketches where mass should go. The HQP then makes forces obey physics. Together they walk fast without falling.

Warning

Never let planning steal the fast loop budget. Run the slow planner on another thread. The 1 kHz loop must always answer on time.

7. Touch gently: impedance, admittance, force

Position control says "go there no matter what". That breaks cups and fingers. Impedance control acts like a soft spring instead. You set stiffness and damping, not just a spot.

Admittance control flips the logic for stiff arms. It feels the push, then moves away gently. Think of a heavy door with a kind motor. Push harder and it yields more.

True force control tracks a push target directly. Polish with 10 N, neither 5 nor 20. It needs a clean force sensor. It also needs friction limits from the QP.

Key idea

Pick stiffness for the job, not the motor. Hard for milling, soft for handshakes. The QP still guards contacts and torque caps.

8. Common mistakes

  • Soft balance. Balance as a low weight drifts and falls. Lock it as a strict rank instead.
  • No regularization. Zero calm cost gives jerky answers. Add a tiny weight on ξ always.
  • Friction ignored. Feet slide when cones are missing. Add cone limits before fast walks.
  • Cold starts only. Solving from zero each tick wastes time. Warm-start from the last ξ.
  • Planner blocks control. A 10 ms plan stalls a 1 ms loop. Split threads and cap solve time.

9. How professionals do it

Pro teams split rates on purpose. The HQP runs at 800 Hz with qpOASES. The SQP planner runs near 100 Hz behind it. Shared memory passes fresh plans without locks.

They log solve time, task errors, and cone margins live. A red tick triggers a safe freeze posture. They replay logs in simulation first. Only clean logs earn hardware time.

They test pushes early and often. A stick shove checks balance ranks fast. Force plots must stay inside cones. Terms live in the glossary so new hires ramp quickly.

10. Try it yourself

  1. List three tasks in rank order. Use a tray-carrying humanoid. Hint: feet first, tray second, gaze last.
  2. Spot the nullspace. Hand draws circles, elbow floats freely. Hint: elbow room is the nullspace.
  3. Set a 1 kHz budget. Split 1.0 ms across sense, solve, send. Hint: give the QP at most 0.8 ms.
  4. Pick soft or strict. Label gaze, balance, and energy saving. Hint: only balance is strict here.

Going deeper (optional): cascaded-fidelity MPC

One model cannot do everything. A full dynamics model is honest but slow. A simple centroidal model is fast but rough. Cascaded-fidelity MPC uses both: a rough planner looks far ahead with the simple model, and a sharp tracker fixes the next steps with the full model.

Think of a road trip. The rough map picks the cities for the week. The sharp map steers each turn today. If the sharp tracker falls behind, the rough planner replans from the true spot. This split is why humanoids now walk at 1.2 m/s and survive 60 N shoves: the planner in Chapter 25 maths runs near 100 Hz while the whole-body QP in this chapter runs near 800 Hz.

Two-rate idea slow planner (simple model, long horizon) → fast tracker (full model, short horizon)
Watch out

The two layers must agree on contacts. If the planner assumes the left foot is planted while the tracker lifts it, the robot falls. Always check contact timing first when a cascade misbehaves.

Key takeaways

  • Task space commands jobs, and nullspace holds leftover freedom.
  • Strict ranks protect balance; soft weights trade comfort goals.
  • One HQP solves accelerations and contact forces together.
  • Fast QP plus slow planner hits real-time budgets.
  • Warm-starts and time caps keep 1 kHz loops alive.
  • Impedance sets softness; force control tracks a push.
