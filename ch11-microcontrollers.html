Microcontrollers and Single-Board Computers

Every robot needs a brain. In this chapter you will learn the two families of small computer that run robots, see what every pin on a board is for, and pick the right board for a real project.

~23 min read Beginner Chapter 11 of 30

What you will learn

  • Tell a microcontroller from a single-board computer.
  • Name the main parts of a dev board.
  • Choose a board for a given project.

Picture a small robot on your desk. It has wheels, a battery, and a sensor that checks for walls. Something inside it must read that sensor, decide to turn, and send power to the motors. That "something" is a tiny computer. This chapter is about choosing and understanding that tiny computer.

A robot without a brain is just metal and wire. The brain is the part that reads the senses and gives the orders. In almost every hobby robot, the brain is one of two things: a microcontroller or a single-board computer. They look similar at first glance. Both are small green boards with metal pins. But they are very different inside, and they suit very different jobs.

By the end of this chapter, you will know exactly which one to reach for. You will also be able to look at any board and name its parts, the way you can name the parts of a bicycle. Let us start with the one idea that makes both of them work: the world of on and off.

The tiny computer inside a robot

A computer does not think the way you do. It only deals with two states: on and off. Engineers write these two states as 1 (on) and 0 (off). This is what we call digital.

Think of a light switch. It is either up or down. There is no "half up". A digital signal is exactly like that. A wire carries either a high voltage (on, a 1) or a low voltage (off, a 0). The computer reads these 0s and 1s and follows rules to make decisions.

The key idea

Digital means a signal that is only ever on or off, never in between. A tiny computer turns the real, smooth world into these two states, makes decisions, and turns them back into actions for motors.

Why does this matter for robots? Because almost every sensor and motor speaks this on/off language, one way or another. A pushed button is on. A light sensor that sees darkness can be turned into a number. A motor driver takes on/off signals and uses them to control speed. Your brain board is the translator in the middle.

Why real things must be turned into numbers

Most things in the real world are smooth, not jumpy. Temperature rises slowly. Light fades gently. A wheel turns faster and faster. These smooth values are called analogue. But a computer only knows 0s and 1s. So something must convert the smooth world into numbers, and numbers back into smooth actions.

That "something" is built into the board. Sensors and special pins do the first half. Motor drivers and other output pins do the second half. You will meet both in this chapter. For now, keep this picture in your head: the real world is smooth, the computer is on/off, and the board is the bridge between them.

In simple words

Digital means "on or off". Analogue means "smooth and changing". Sensors usually give analogue signals. The board changes them to numbers so the program can use them.

Microcontrollers: one chip, one program, forever

A microcontroller — often shortened to MCU — is a whole small computer squeezed onto a single chip. It has a processor to do the thinking, a tiny amount of memory, and special hardware for pins, all in one piece of silicon.

An MCU is built for one job. You write one program, upload it to the chip, and the chip runs that program forever, from the moment power arrives. There is no desktop, no login, no windows. Just your instructions, repeating in a loop.

What an MCU is good at

The MCU shines at a few things:

  • Real-time control. It reacts in tiny, exact slices of time — often thousands of times per second.
  • Low power. Many run happily on a small battery for hours or days.
  • Starting instantly. Power on and it is ready in a fraction of a second.
  • Simple and cheap. A basic board can cost less than a sandwich.

Real-time means the board can promise to respond within a fixed, known time. If a sensor says "the edge of the table is here", the MCU can cut the motor power within a few thousandths of a second, every single time. This promise is why MCUs run the moving parts of robots.

Note

A development board ("dev board") is the green board you hold, not the chip alone. The board adds the parts you need to use the chip easily: a USB socket, a power regulator, a reset button, and rows of pins. The chip is the brain; the board is the brain's desk, chair and phone.

Single-board computers: a whole computer on one board

A single-board computer — or SBC — is a different animal. It is a complete computer, with all its main parts on one board: processor, memory, storage slot, USB ports, an HDMI socket for a screen, and a network socket or Wi-Fi. The most famous is the Raspberry Pi.

The big difference is the operating system. An SBC runs a full operating system, usually a version of Linux. That means it can run many programs at once, show a desktop, browse the web, plug in a camera, and store files, just like a small laptop.

What an SBC is good at

  • Heavy thinking. It has far more memory and speed for tasks like image recognition.
  • Cameras and vision. It can plug in a camera and run computer-vision software in real time.
  • Networking. Wi-Fi, Ethernet, and the ability to talk to the internet easily.
  • Many programs at once. You can run a camera viewer, a web server and your robot code together.

Famous SBCs include the Raspberry Pi (the friendly all-rounder), and the NVIDIA Jetson family (built for AI and vision, with a powerful graphics chip onboard). There are many others, but these two names will carry you a long way.

The catch: no real-time promise

An SBC runs an operating system that shares the processor between many jobs. It cannot promise to react in a fixed tiny time. A virus scan, a network message, or the desktop can steal a turn and make your robot code late. So an SBC is wonderful for thinking, but poor at split-second control of motors.

Watch out

Do not expect a Raspberry Pi to be real-time. Under load, a Pi can be busy for many milliseconds and miss a sensor reading. For tasks where timing must be exact — motor control, balancing, flight — use a microcontroller.

Booting up: a story of two speeds

When you switch on an MCU, your program is already in its flash memory and it starts in a fraction of a second. When you switch on an SBC, you must wait for the operating system to load. This takes many seconds, sometimes a minute on slower cards. For a robot that should start moving the instant you flick a switch, that wait matters.

The parts of a development board, pin by pin

Now let us open the hood. Take any dev board in your hand and you will find the same family of parts. Figure 11.2 shows a typical Arduino-style board. We will walk around it, group by group, in plain words.

The microcontroller chip

The black rectangle is the chip itself. It runs your program and controls every pin. Everything else on the board exists to feed it, protect it, or connect it to the outside world.

Power pins (5V, 3.3V, GND)

These pins give out electricity, or provide the return path. 5V and 3.3V are supply pins at those voltages. GND ("ground") is the common return line — think of it as the "minus" side that everything shares. Every sensor needs power and ground to work.

Digital I/O pins

I/O means input/output. A digital pin can only be high (on) or low (off). Use it to read a button, or to switch an LED. There are only two states, but that is often all you need.

Analogue input pins

Analogue pins read a smooth voltage and turn it into a number. This is how many sensors report the world: a light sensor, a temperature sensor, and a distance sensor all give a voltage that rises and falls. The board converts it to a number your program can compare.

PWM pins

PWM stands for pulse-width modulation. It is a clever trick that makes a digital pin behave a little like a smooth output. We will explain it fully in its own section, because it is how you control motor speed and servo angle.

Communication pins (UART, I2C, SPI)

These pins let the board talk to other chips and modules. There are three main languages, and you will learn each one soon: UART (the simple one-to-one chat), I2C (a shared bus for many sensors), and SPI (the fast one for displays and memory cards).

USB port

The USB socket does two jobs. It powers the board while you work at your desk, and it carries your program into the chip. On most starter boards, you upload code and get debugging messages over this same cable.

Reset button

The reset button restarts your program from the top. It is the same as flicking the power off and on, but faster. You will press it constantly while learning.

On-board LED

Most boards have a small light wired to a fixed pin. It is the easiest way to check that your code is running. Blinking this LED is the "hello world" of robotics.

Voltage regulator

The regulator takes the incoming voltage — say 5V from USB, or 9V from a battery — and produces the steady lower voltage the chip needs. It protects the chip from a supply that is too high or unsteady.

Clock or crystal

Every computer needs a heartbeat to keep every step in time. A small silver component, the crystal, provides it: millions of ticks per second. This heartbeat is why an MCU can keep time so exactly.

Bootloader

The bootloader is a tiny helper program already burned into the chip. When you upload code from your computer, the bootloader receives it and writes it into flash memory. You never see it, but it is what makes "click upload" work.

Digital, analogue and PWM

It is worth slowing down here, because these three ideas explain most of the pins on a board.

Digital pins: two states only

A digital pin is a switch. Read it, and you see on or off. Write to it, and you turn something on or off. A push button, a bump switch, and a simple light are all digital. Digital pins are simple and very reliable.

Analogue pins: a whole range of values

An analogue pin measures a voltage and gives you a number. On many boards the number runs from 0 to 1023 (that is 10 bits, or 1024 steps). A reading near 0 means almost no voltage; a reading near 1023 means almost full voltage. In between, you get everything in the middle.

This matters because most sensors do not just say "dark" or "bright". They say "this bright". A light sensor, a bendy flex sensor, a spin knob, and the classic ultrasonic and infrared distance sensors all report a smooth value. Analogue pins are how your robot hears these gentle signals.

Note

The job of turning a smooth voltage into a number is called analogue-to-digital conversion, or ADC. The ADC is built into the chip. That is why analogue pins exist at all.

PWM: making a digital pin feel analogue

Some pins can do a trick called PWM (pulse-width modulation). The pin still switches fully on and fully off, but it switches so fast that the "on" time can be varied. Switch on for a tiny slice of each moment, and the average is low. Switch on most of the time, and the average is high.

Think of blinking a lamp very fast. If it is on for a tenth of each second, your eye sees a dim glow. If it is on for nine tenths, it looks bright. PWM does exactly this with electricity. It lets a plain on/off pin pretend to be a smooth dimmer.

PWM duty cycle Average = Supply × (On time ÷ Total period)

That fraction — on time divided by total period — is called the duty cycle. A 25% duty cycle on a 5V pin gives an average of about 1.25V. This is wonderful for two jobs in robotics: motor speed and servo angle.

  • Motor speed. A 30% duty cycle turns a motor slowly; 90% turns it fast. The motor sees the average, not the fast flicker.
  • Servo angle. A servo is a motor that moves to a commanded angle. It expects a pulse of a certain width: about 1 millisecond for one end, 1.5 ms for the middle, 2 ms for the other end, repeating about 50 times per second. PWM creates those pulses.
In simple words

PWM is fast on-and-off switching. Change how long it stays on, and you change the average power. That controls how fast a motor spins or which way a servo points.

Voltage levels: the 3.3V and 5V trap

Not all boards use the same "on" voltage. Older and many Arduino-style boards treat 5V as a logic high. Many newer boards, including the Raspberry Pi Pico, the ESP32 and most Raspberry Pis, treat 3.3V as high. This small difference causes more beginner heartbreak than any other topic.

If you feed a 5V signal into a pin that only expects 3.3V, you may damage that pin, or the whole chip. The pin is not built for the higher voltage. Think of it as blowing too hard into a balloon made for a gentle puff.

Danger

Never connect a 5V sensor output straight to a 3.3V input pin. It can kill the pin or the chip instantly. Always check the voltage of both sides before you connect the wire.

How to fix it: level shifting

The safe fix is called level shifting. A level shifter is a tiny part that translates between 5V and 3.3V in either direction. You connect the low side to the 3.3V board and the high side to the 5V device, and it safely passes signals across. Cheap modules exist for exactly this reason.

There is also a simple resistor trick for one-way signals going from a 5V device down to a 3.3V input. But the cleanest, safest habit for a beginner is: when in doubt, use a level shifter, or choose sensors that already match your board's voltage.

Powering your board

A board needs power, and there are three common sources during a project.

  1. USB. Easiest for learning. It gives about 5V and a limited current. Perfect on the bench; not enough to drive motors.
  2. Battery. Needed for a moving robot. A battery pack gives higher voltage (say 6V to 12V) and more current.
  3. Regulator on the board. Takes the battery voltage and steadies it down to the 5V or 3.3V the chip wants.

Watch the current limits

Every board pin can only supply a small current — often about 20 to 40 milliamps (mA) per pin, with a total limit for the whole chip. A motor wants hundreds of milliamps, or several amps, to spin. A pin cannot feed a motor directly.

Worse, a board's own regulator can only pass so much current across the whole board. If you make it feed motors, the voltage can sag, the chip can reset, and your robot behaves strangely. The next section explains the fix.

Keep motor power and logic power separate

Professionals follow one golden rule: give the motors their own power path. You run the battery to a motor driver, which is a board that takes weak signals from the MCU and switches strong current to the motors. The MCU and its sensors get their own clean, steady supply.

The two supplies share a common ground so signals can be understood, but they do not fight over current. When a motor suddenly draws a big gulp of current, it does not pull the chip's voltage down, and the chip keeps running.

Power budget example P = V × I

Let us use that formula. Power (P, in watts) equals voltage (V) times current (I, in amps). Imagine a robot with two small motors. Each motor draws 0.25A at 6V while running. The board and sensors draw 0.1A at 5V. Add it up:

  • Motors: 0.25A × 6V = 1.5W each, so 1.5 × 2 = 3.0W.
  • Board and sensors: 0.1A × 5V = 0.5W.
  • Total: about 3.5W while moving.

Now, if the battery gives 6V 2A, it can supply up to 12W — plenty. But notice the motors use six times the power of the brain. That is why the motor supplies are separate and stronger. When you add a second motor or a bigger load, this little sum tells you instantly whether your supply is strong enough.

Tip

Always size your battery for the worst case: everything running at once, including any stall current when a wheel is stuck. A stuck motor can draw several times its normal current.

Talking to other chips: UART, I2C and SPI

Your board rarely works alone. It talks to sensors, displays, memory cards and even other boards. There are three main ways to talk, and each has a character of its own.

UART (serial): the simple one-to-one chat

UART, also called serial, is the simplest language. Two pins, one for sending and one for receiving. Two devices talk directly, like two people on a phone call. It is easy and it travels a decent distance. It is the first language you meet, because your USB upload often becomes a serial message you can watch on your computer.

I2C: a bus for many sensors

I2C is a shared bus. One pair of wires carries a whole group of devices, like a party line where everyone listens to the same wire. Each device has its own address, a number that acts like a house number. When the board calls address 0x68, only that device answers. This is perfect when you have several small sensors, such as a compass, a colour sensor and a barometer, and want to use just two pins for all of them.

SPI: the fast one

SPI is the quickest of the three. It uses more wires (a clock, a data-out, a data-in, and one "chip select" line per device), but it can move large amounts of data very fast. Use SPI for things that need speed: a colour display, an SD memory card, or a radio module.

In simple words

UART is a phone call between two chips. I2C is a party line where each guest has a number. SPI is a fast private line with extra wires. Pick by how many devices you need and how fast the data must flow.

How a board runs your program

You write a program on a big computer, then move it onto the little board. Here is the whole journey, step by step.

  1. Write. You type code in an editor, often using the words setup() and loop().
  2. Upload. You connect USB and click upload. The editor compiles your code into machine instructions.
  3. Transfer. The bootloader on the chip receives the instructions and writes them into flash memory.
  4. Run on power-up. Every time the board gets power, the chip reads the program from flash and starts it.
  5. Loop forever. The code in loop() repeats endlessly until power is removed or the reset button is pressed.

Flash memory is special: it keeps its contents even when the power is off. That is why your robot still remembers its program after you unplug the battery. The chip's regular working memory, called RAM, is erased when power goes away — it is just a scratchpad while the program runs.

Arduino C++ 
void setup() {
  // runs ONCE at power-up
  pinMode(13, OUTPUT);   // pin 13 drives the on-board LED
}

void loop() {
  // runs FOREVER, over and over
  digitalWrite(13, HIGH);  // LED on
  delay(500);              // wait 500 milliseconds
  digitalWrite(13, LOW);   // LED off
  delay(500);              // wait again
}

Read it in plain English. "At start-up, make pin 13 an output. Then, forever: turn the light on, wait half a second, turn it off, wait half a second." That tiny loop is the heartbeat of every robot program. You will grow it until it reads sensors, decides, and drives motors.

Note

On an MCU, the program is ready in a fraction of a second because there is no operating system to load. On an SBC, your program waits while Linux starts, which can take many seconds. This is one more reason MCUs feel "instant".

Choosing a board: a step-by-step method

People often ask "which board should I buy?" The honest answer is "it depends on the job". Here is a method you can use every time, in order. Stop at the first question that gives you a strong answer.

  1. Do I need a camera or heavy vision? If yes, lean toward an SBC (a Raspberry Pi, or a Jetson for AI).
  2. Do I need split-second, repeatable timing? If yes, you need an MCU, or an MCU working alongside an SBC.
  3. What is my power budget? Battery and long run time favour an MCU. Fewer watts mean smaller batteries.
  4. What is my cost limit? MCU boards are cheapest. SBCs cost more, and AI boards most of all.
  5. How much help do I need? Beginners thrive on boards with big communities, like Arduino and Raspberry Pi.
  6. Do I have enough pins and features? Count your sensors and motors first, then check Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and camera needs.
  7. Match the voltage. Know whether your sensors are 3.3V or 5V, and whether you need level shifting.
Tip

When you are unsure, start with one MCU board and one SBC. The MCU teaches you real-time control; the SBC teaches you Linux and vision. Together they cover nearly every project.

Worked scenario 1: a line-following car

Job: drive along a black line, stop at the end. Decision: no camera, needs fast repeatable timing for the motors, runs on a battery, low cost. Choice: an MCU such as an Arduino Uno or an ESP32. The sensor is analogue or digital, the motors need PWM, and the loop must run thousands of times a second.

Worked scenario 2: a camera that follows a coloured ball

Job: see a ball with a camera and steer toward it. Decision: needs vision and a camera port, and can be mains powered. Choice: an SBC such as a Raspberry Pi. The camera and vision software need Linux and lots of memory. If the wheels must react in strict real time, add a small MCU to drive the motors, with the Pi sending it commands.

Worked scenario 3: a battery greenhouse monitor

Job: wake up every ten minutes, read temperature and humidity, post the numbers to the internet. Decision: tiny power use, one sensor, needs Wi-Fi. Choice: an MCU with Wi-Fi, such as an ESP32. It sleeps most of the time, wakes to read the sensor, sends the data, and sleeps again — so one small battery lasts a long time.

A mini worked example: wiring a sensor and a motor driver

Let us wire two real parts to an Arduino-style board: an ultrasonic distance sensor and a motor driver. We will choose each pin and say why.

The sensor: an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor has four pins: VCC (power), Trig (trigger), Echo (reply) and GND. It works by sending a short "ping" of sound and timing how long the echo takes to return.

The driver: an L298N motor driver has pins for two motors and a set of control inputs for direction and speed.

  1. Power the sensor. Connect VCC to the board's 5V pin and GND to a GND pin. Reason: the HC-SR04 wants 5V, so use a 5V board or level shifting if your board is 3.3V.
  2. Connect Trig to a digital output pin (say pin 9). Reason: the board sends a short pulse out; that is an output job.
  3. Connect Echo to a digital input pin (say pin 10). Reason: the board listens for the reply; that is an input job. Note the Echo pin returns 5V, so a 3.3V board needs a level shifter here.
  4. Connect the motor driver's VCC terminal to the battery, not to the board's 5V pin. Reason: motors need far more current than a board pin can safely give.
  5. Connect the driver's ground and the board's ground together. Reason: both sides must share the same ground so the signals mean the same thing.
  6. Connect the driver's direction pins to two digital output pins (say IN1 and IN2). Reason: the board sets these high or low to choose forward or backward.
  7. Connect the driver's speed pin (ENA) to a PWM-capable pin (say pin 6). Reason: PWM sets the motor speed smoothly.
  8. Connect the motor wires to the driver's OUT terminals. Then test with the wheels off the ground before the robot touches the floor.

Why this wiring is good: sensors are powered from the board's clean 5V, while motors are powered from the battery through the driver. The two grounds are joined so signals agree. The trigger uses an output pin, the echo uses an input pin, direction uses plain digital pins, and speed uses a PWM pin. Every choice matches the job the pin does — and that is the whole skill of wiring.

Danger

Never power a motor from a board pin or from the board's 5V pin. The current will overheat the regulator or reset the chip. Always send motor power through a driver, from the battery.

Common mistakes beginners make

These are the errors that appear again and again. Read them now so you can dodge them later.

  • Connecting a 5V signal to a 3.3V pin. This is the number one board killer. Always check both voltages and use a level shifter when they differ.
  • Powering motors from the board. Motors pull far too much current. Use a driver and a separate battery path. Keep the grounds joined.
  • Running out of pins. Count your sensors and motors before you buy a board. If a project needs many sensors, prefer I2C so many sensors share two pins.
  • Expecting a Pi to be real-time. An SBC shares its processor and can be late. For exact timing, use an MCU.
  • Forgetting the common ground. If two boards do not share a ground, their signals have no shared reference and behave randomly.
  • Assuming all USB cables carry data. Some cheap cables only charge. If upload fails, try a known-good data cable before anything else.
  • Ignoring the current your supply must give. Do the power budget sum with the worst case, including stalled motors.

How professionals do it

At industrial scale, the choices become more careful, but the same ideas still hold. Here is what changes.

MCU and SBC working together

Many real robots use both brains. The SBC is the strategist: it processes the camera, plans the route and talks to the network. A small MCU is the reflex: it drives the motors, reads the safety sensors and reacts in real time. The two talk over a serial link. If the SBC is busy, the MCU still keeps the robot safe and moving. You will meet this pairing in a later chapter on system design.

Real-time operating systems (RTOS)

A plain MCU program runs its loop in order. A real-time operating system, or RTOS, lets one MCU run several tasks with timing promises: one task for motors, one for the radio, one for sensors. The RTOS decides whose turn is next so every deadline is met. It is the middle ground between "one simple program" and "a full Linux computer".

Custom circuit boards

A hobbyist plugs wires between ready-made boards. A professional designs a single PCB (printed circuit board) that holds the chip, the sensors and the connectors as one neat unit. That removes loose wires, saves space and weight, and is far more reliable in a factory or a product.

Safety and watchdog timers

What if the software hangs? Professionals keep a watchdog timer running. It is a small clock that expects the program to "pet" it regularly. If the program freezes and stops petting it, the watchdog automatically resets the board. This one habit turns a silent failure into a recoverable one. Safety of people always comes before cleverness.

The professional habit

Use an MCU for anything that must be safe and on time. Use an SBC for anything that must see, plan or connect. When a robot needs both, give it both and let them talk.

Try it yourself

These exercises need little or no hardware. They build your judgment, which is the real skill.

  1. Sort the job. For each task, write "MCU" or "SBC" and one reason: (a) blink an LED, (b) recognise a face, (c) keep a robot balanced, (d) host a camera stream. Hint: timing favours MCU; vision favours SBC.
  2. Count your pins. Plan a robot with two motors, an ultrasonic sensor and a push button. List every pin you need. Hint: motors need direction pins plus a PWM speed pin each.
  3. Do a power budget. A robot has two 0.3A motors at 6V and a 0.15A board at 5V. Using P = V × I, find the total watts while moving. Hint: two motors first, then add the board.
  4. Spot the voltage trap. You have a 3.3V board and a sensor that outputs 5V. Write the safe way to connect them. Hint: level shifter, low side to the board.
  5. Choose a language. You want to connect four small sensors using only two pins. Which of UART, I2C or SPI fits best, and why? Hint: many devices, few pins, each with an address.

Going deeper (optional)

If you are curious, one advanced idea is worth a mention. On an MCU you can use interrupts. An interrupt tells the chip "stop the main loop for a moment and handle this important event now", such as a wheel encoder tick or a button press. Interrupts let a small chip react to fast events without missing them. Combined with an RTOS and a watchdog timer, they are how professional firmware stays both fast and safe. You do not need them yet, but the name will not surprise you when you meet it.

Key takeaways

  • Digital means on or off (1 or 0); analogue means smooth and changing.
  • A microcontroller is one chip that runs one program forever, in real time, on very little power.
  • A single-board computer is a full computer with an operating system, best for cameras, networks and heavy thinking.
  • A dev board adds USB power, a regulator, a reset button and pins around the chip.
  • Analogue pins read smooth sensors; PWM fakes a smooth output for motor speed and servo angle.
  • Never mix 5V and 3.3V signals without level shifting, and never power motors from a board pin.
  • Choose by the job: timing and motors mean an MCU; vision and planning mean an SBC.

Q4.Why can a Raspberry Pi not be trusted for strict real-time motor control?
Answer. Its operating system shares the processor between many jobs, so under load its robot code can be delayed. A microcontroller gives a fixed, reliable timing promise instead.
Q5.What is PWM, and name two jobs it does in robotics.
Answer. PWM is fast on-and-off switching whose on-time fraction sets the average power. It controls motor speed and sets a servo's angle.
Q6.You have a 5V sensor output and a 3.3V board input. What should you add, and why?
Answer. A level shifter. It safely lowers the 5V signal to 3.3V so it does not damage the pin or the chip.
Q7.Why must motors get power through a driver and not from a board pin?
Answer. Motors draw far more current than a pin can supply. A driver switches strong battery current, while the board only sends weak control signals, keeping the chip safe.
Q8.A project needs a camera to spot a person and wheels that stop within a few milliseconds. Which board or boards would you choose, and how would they work together?
Answer. Use both. An SBC (like a Raspberry Pi) runs the camera and vision to spot the person; a small MCU drives the motors to stop in real time. They talk over a serial link so the fast reflexes stay on the MCU even if the SBC is busy.
