Tools and Materials for Robotics

A robot is a machine you build with your hands before you ever write code. This chapter shows you which tools to buy, which materials to choose, and how to set up a bench where good work happens safely.

~19 min read Beginner Chapter 4 of 30

What you will learn

  • List the core hand tools a beginner really needs.
  • Match materials such as aluminium, plastic and plywood to jobs.
  • Set up a tidy, safe workbench.

Picture a small robot on your desk. It rolls forward, sees a wall, and turns away. That machine did not appear by magic. Somebody measured, cut, drilled and screwed it together. Before you write a single line of code, you need a place to work and a few good tools.

Many beginners start by buying an expensive kit. Then they get stuck the first time a screw does not fit, or a part needs a small hole. A kit teaches you to follow steps. Tools teach you to solve problems. This chapter gives you the second skill.

Here is the good news. You do not need a big workshop. You do not need to spend a lot. You need a small set of tools that work, a few materials you understand, and a bench you keep tidy. Let us build that step by step.

Your first toolkit, grouped by job

A tool has one job, and it does that job well. When you group tools by job, you stop asking "what should I buy?" and start asking "what job do I need to do today?"

We will walk through six groups. Together they cover almost every beginner robot task: measuring, cutting, holding, fastening, electronics and power tools. At the end we add the small consumables that get used up.

Measuring tools — find the true size

Every robot part must fit another part. Fitting starts with measuring. A ruler or steel rule is enough for rough sizes. It is cheap and always useful. Read the marks carefully, because the small lines are millimetres.

A digital caliper is the star of measuring. A caliper is a sliding tool with two jaws. You clamp the jaws around a part and read the size on a screen. Good calipers read to 0.01 mm. That tiny precision sounds extreme, but you will see in a moment why it matters.

Add a small try square to check that corners are truly 90 degrees. A square is an L-shaped tool. Press it into a corner, and any gap means the corner is not square.

Cutting tools — change the shape

Cutting removes material so a part takes the shape you want. Start with side cutters. These are small pliers that snip wire, cable ties and thin plastic. They are not for steel.

A hacksaw is a hand saw with a fine blade held in a frame. It cuts metal rod, aluminium bar and plastic pipe. Use slow, steady strokes. Let the blade do the work instead of pushing hard.

A craft knife cuts cardboard, foam board and tape. It is sharp and unforgiving. Always cut away from your body, and always put the blade away when you are done.

Watch out

A blunt blade is more dangerous than a sharp one. You push harder, the blade slips, and you cut yourself. Change blades often. A ten-second blade swap protects your fingers.

Holding tools — keep parts still

You cannot drill, cut or solder a part that slides around. Holding tools solve this. A vice clamps to the bench and grips a part while both your hands stay free. A clamp does the same job for larger or awkward parts.

Tweezers hold very small things: tiny screws, short wires, surface-mount parts. Good tweezers have a fine, firm tip. They are the difference between patience and frustration when you work on small electronics.

Fastening tools — join parts together

Robots are held together with screws, bolts and nuts. Screwdrivers come in two kinds: flat and cross-shaped (Phillips). Buy a set with several sizes, not one cheap driver.

Hex keys, also called Allen keys, turn screws with a six-sided hole in the head. Most robot kits use these, because a hex key reaches into tight corners. Spanners grip nuts and bolts. Nut drivers are like screwdrivers but with a socket on the end, so they hold a nut without slipping.

In simple words

A screwdriver turns a screw. A spanner turns a nut. A hex key turns a screw with a six-sided hole. Each one fits a different shape of head, so you need the right one for the job.

Electronics tools — work with power safely

Robots mix metal with electricity, so you need a few electronic tools. A multimeter measures voltage, current and resistance. It tells you whether a battery is charged, whether a wire is broken, and whether power reaches a motor.

A soldering iron melts metal to join wires and components. Heat is its job, so treat it with respect. A wire stripper removes the plastic coat from a wire without cutting the metal inside. A breadboard lets you build circuits by pushing wires into holes, with no soldering at all.

Power tools — faster, but louder and sharper

A drill makes holes and drives screws. A rotary tool is a small hand-held motor that spins cutters and grinding tips. It can cut, sand and polish in tight places.

Power tools make a good job fast and a careless job dangerous. Safety comes first, not last. Wear eye protection. Clamp your work. Keep loose hair and sleeves away from anything that spins. Never rush a power tool.

Danger

A drill bit or cutting disc can throw small sharp pieces into the air. Always wear safety glasses when you drill, cut or grind. This is not optional. Eyes do not heal like skin.

Consumables — the things you use up

Consumables are not tools, because they get used and replaced. Keep a small stock, so a job never stops because a roll ran out.

  • Tape — paper tape and electrical tape for holding and insulating.
  • Heat-shrink — a plastic tube that shrinks around a wire join when heated, covering the bare metal.
  • Cable ties — the quickest way to hold wires and cables in place.
  • Threadlock — a liquid that stops screws from shaking loose, common on moving robots.
  • Sandpaper — smooths rough edges so they do not cut you or a wire.
  • Glue — superglue for small quick joins, and epoxy for strong permanent joins.

Buy this first, buy this later

You will see long lists of tools online. Do not buy them all at once. Buy in two waves, so your money follows your skills.

Buy first means the tools you will use in your first week. They are cheap and useful for almost any job. Buy later means the tools you grow into once you know what you like to build.

Materials: choose the right stuff

Tools let you shape a robot. Materials decide how the robot behaves. Pick the wrong material and the robot is too heavy, too weak, or too expensive. In this section, you learn to match a material to a job.

There are three questions to ask about any material:

  1. How strong is it? Will it bend or break under force?
  2. How heavy is it? Extra weight needs bigger motors.
  3. How easy is it to work? Can you cut it with your tools?

We will look at metals first, then plastics, then wood and board, and finally 3D-printing filament.

Aluminium — the robot builder's favourite

Aluminium is a light metal. It is about one-third as heavy as steel but still quite strong. It is soft enough to cut with hand tools, yet stiff enough to hold a robot's shape.

It also machines well. "Machines well" means a drill or cutter makes clean cuts in it. It does not grab the bit or chip badly. This is why so many robot frames are aluminium.

Use aluminium for chassis plates (the flat base of a robot), motor mounts, brackets and arms. It is the default choice when you want strength without much weight.

Steel — very strong but heavy

Steel is an iron metal that is much stronger than aluminium. It resists bending and wear very well. But it is heavy, and it is hard to cut or drill with hand tools.

Use steel for shafts, bearings, springs and parts that take heavy wear. Do not build a whole small robot from steel unless the robot must be very strong. The weight would force you to buy large motors.

Plastics — light, cheap and easy to shape

Plastics are made from long chemical chains. They are light, cheap, and easy to cut, drill and glue. Each plastic has a different personality.

  • ABS — a tough everyday plastic used in LEGO bricks and many 3D prints. It is strong, but it gives off fumes when melted, so print it in a ventilated room.
  • PLA — the easiest 3D-printing plastic. It is cheap and prints cleanly, but it is brittle and softens in heat. Great for models and indoor parts.
  • PETG — like PLA but much tougher and more heat-resistant. It is the best all-round printing plastic for parts that get used.
  • Acrylic — a clear, stiff plastic that looks like glass. It scratches easily and cracks under sharp impact, but it is lovely for guards and display panels.
  • Polycarbonate — the best plastic for toughness. It bends and absorbs hits instead of shattering. Use it where a part must survive crashes.
The key idea

Match the material to the job, not to your mood. Light and stiff for frames. Tough for bumpers. Cheap and fast for prototypes. Strong and hard for wear parts.

Wood and board — cheap and quick

Plywood is thin layers of wood glued at right angles. It is strong for its weight, cheap, and easy to cut with a saw. It makes excellent prototype chassis and jigs.

MDF is a board of pressed wood fibres. It is smooth and cheap, but it is heavy and swells if it gets wet. Use it for test shapes, not for a finished outdoor robot.

Foam board is a foam sheet with paper on both sides. It is feather-light and easy to cut with a craft knife. It is perfect for quick models to check size and shape before you cut expensive metal.

3D-printing filament — custom shapes on demand

Filament is the plastic string a 3D printer melts into shapes. You design a part on a computer, and the printer builds it layer by layer. This lets you make brackets and mounts that would be hard to cut by hand.

Start with PLA while you learn. Move to PETG for parts that must be tough or warm. Keep polycarbonate for the parts that must survive heavy hits, once you can print it well.

Fasteners: screws, nuts and more

A fastener is anything that holds two parts together. Screws and bolts do most of this work. Choosing the right fastener is as important as choosing the right material.

Most small robots use metric fasteners. Metric means the size is measured in millimetres. A screw is named by its diameter and its pitch. For example, M3 means a screw 3 mm across, and M4 means 4 mm across.

Pitch: the hidden number

Pitch is the distance between two screw threads. Think of the ridges on a screw as a spiral staircase. Pitch is the gap between one step and the next. A common M3 screw has a pitch of 0.5 mm.

Thread pitch pitch = distance between adjacent thread peaks (in mm)

Why does pitch matter? Two screws can share the same diameter but have a different pitch. A fine-pitch screw will not fit a coarse-pitch nut. If a nut feels tight after one turn, you likely have the wrong pitch. Stop before you damage the threads.

Why M3 is the robotics standard

M3 screws are the most common size in small robotics. They are strong enough to hold a motor, yet small enough to fit tight spaces. They are cheap and sold everywhere. When you design your own parts, M3 is the safe default.

M4 screws are for parts that take more force: bigger motors, heavier arms, supports. M2 screws appear in tiny electronics, where space is very small.

The fastener family

  • Nuts — a flat ring with a threaded hole. It screws onto a bolt to trap the parts.
  • Nyloc nuts — a nut with a nylon ring inside. The ring grips the bolt, so vibration cannot loosen it. Ideal for wheels and joints.
  • Washers — thin rings that spread the load of a screw head and stop it digging into soft material.
  • Standoffs — small posts with threads at each end. They hold two boards apart at a fixed distance.
  • Rivets — a fastener that expands to lock two sheets together permanently. Quick and light, but hard to remove.
  • Zip ties — plastic straps that hold cables and light parts. Great for tidy wiring, weak for structure.
Note

Bolts and screws are cousins. A bolt usually passes all the way through and is held by a nut. A screw often cuts its own thread into the material. Both are fasteners, and both use the same naming rules.

Choosing a material: a simple method

Beginners freeze when they must choose a material. Instead of guessing, follow five short questions. Ask them in order. The answer usually becomes obvious.

  1. Load. How much force will the part carry? A tiny sensor mount carries almost none. A wheel bracket carries the whole robot.
  2. Weight. How much does this part add? Lighter parts let smaller motors move the robot. Heavy parts demand bigger motors.
  3. Cost. How much can you spend? Cheap materials let you build more and make mistakes. Save expensive material for final parts.
  4. Tools. What can you actually cut and drill? If you only have hand tools, avoid steel. Plywood and plastic are easy.
  5. Environment. Where will the robot live? Water, heat, sunlight and repeated crashes all change the answer. Use tough polycarbonate outdoors, not brittle acrylic.

Worked through, these questions take about one minute. That minute saves you from a heavy, expensive robot you cannot move.

Tip

Build the first version in cheap material. Test it. Only then cut the part in aluminium. Prototype in cardboard or foam, and you will rarely waste metal.

Measuring and tolerances

Robotics is a game of small distances. A gap of 0.1 mm can decide whether a shaft spins freely or jams. This is why a caliper belongs in your first toolkit.

A tolerance is how much a real part is allowed to differ from the size you wanted. If you design a hole at 6 mm and allow a tolerance of 0.1 mm, the real hole may be between 5.9 mm and 6.1 mm. Tolerances let parts still fit when they are made imperfectly.

Clearance fit for a shaft in a hole hole_size − shaft_size = clearance

For a bearing to spin smoothly, the shaft must be slightly smaller than the hole. Too tight and it jams. Too loose and it wobbles. A clearance of about 0.1 mm is often a good starting point.

Using a caliper well

  1. Close the jaws fully and check the screen reads zero. If not, press the zero button.
  2. Open the jaws wider than the part.
  3. Close them gently onto the part. Do not force them.
  4. Read the number. For a round shaft, measure at three places and rotate the part, to find the true size.
  5. Write the number down right away. Do not trust your memory.

Remember the old workshop rule: measure twice, cut once. Measuring costs seconds. Cutting a part too short costs a new piece of material and your afternoon.

In simple words

A tolerance is the "wiggle room" you allow. A clearance is the small gap that lets one part move inside another. Both are measured in very small amounts, like 0.1 mm.

Setting up your workbench

A good workbench makes work easy. A bad one makes every job a struggle. You do not need a big room. You need good habits built into the layout.

Divide the bench into zones

Think of your bench as four zones. Each zone holds one kind of activity, so your hands always know where to reach.

  1. Build zone. The open centre where you cut, drill and assemble. Keep it clear.
  2. Test zone. Space for the laptop, the multimeter and a power supply, so you can check circuits.
  3. Tool zone. A wall board or tray near your dominant hand. Tools go back after every use.
  4. Storage zone. Bins and drawers for screws, wire and spare parts.

Light and power

Good light prevents mistakes. Put a bright, adjustable lamp over the build zone. Shadows hide small parts and cause bad cuts. If you solder, aim the light past your hand, not into your eyes.

Power needs care. Use a socket strip with a switch, mounted on the wall, not on the wet-prone bench. Never overload a strip. Keep drinks away from every electric tool.

ESD basics

ESD means electrostatic discharge. It is the tiny spark you feel when you touch a door handle. That spark can be thousands of volts, and it can silently damage electronic parts.

You do not need a full lab setup as a beginner. Keep three habits: touch a metal water pipe or a grounded bench leg before handling circuit boards, store boards in anti-static bags, and avoid working on carpet in dry air.

Watch out

You cannot always see ESD damage. A board may work today and fail tomorrow. Protect parts before you touch them, not after they fail.

Labelling and storage

Small parts vanish. A screw the size of a grain of rice will roll under anything. Fix this with storage bins. Use clear bins or drawers, one size or type per bin. Write a label on each bin.

Label everything: draw the bin name on paper and tape it on. Label cables at both ends with tape flags. When you return to a project after a week, labels bring you back fast.

Cable management keeps the bench safe. Route wires away from the cutting area. Coil spare cable and hold it with a zip tie. A tidy bench is a fast bench and a safe bench.

Worked example: a two-wheel robot chassis

Let us put everything together. Your goal is a small two-wheel robot. Two motors drive two wheels, and a third point keeps it level. You must choose tools and materials and explain every choice.

Step 1 — Set the requirements

The robot is small, maybe 150 mm across. It carries a battery, a controller board, a distance sensor and two motors. It will run indoors on a smooth floor. You want it cheap and easy to rebuild.

Step 2 — Choose the material for the base

Ask the five questions. The load is light. Weight matters, because small motors are weak. Cost should be low. Your tools are hand tools. The floor is dry and warm.

Plywood wins for version one. It is cheap, easy to cut, and light enough. Aluminium would also work, but it costs more and needs a drill. Keep aluminium for the final version.

Step 3 — Choose the material for the motor mounts

Motor mounts take a real load, and they must be accurate. Here, a printed part in PETG is ideal. You design the mount once, and the printer makes it exact. PETG is tough enough for a small robot.

If you have no 3D printer, buy aluminium motor brackets. They are cheap and strong. Both answers are good. The point is that different parts can use different materials.

Step 4 — Choose the fasteners

Use M3 screws for the motors and the board. M3 is small, strong and everywhere. Add a washer under each screw head so it does not crush the plywood. Use nyloc nuts on the motor mounts, because motors vibrate.

Step 5 — Choose the tools

  1. Steel rule and caliper — mark the base size and check the wheel spacing.
  2. Craft knife and small saw — cut the plywood to shape.
  3. Drill — make the M3 holes for the motors and board.
  4. Hex keys and screwdriver — assemble the parts.
  5. Sandpaper — smooth the cut edges so they do not cut wires.
  6. Multimeter — check the battery and wiring before you power up.

Step 6 — Check the fit before you commit

Cut the base from foam board first. Place the motors, battery and board on it. Does everything fit? Are the wheels clear of the base? Move parts around until the layout is good. Only then cut the plywood.

This step is the single biggest time-saver in the whole build. A foam-board model costs pennies. A wrong plywood base costs an hour.

The key idea

Prototype in cheap stuff, then build in the real material. This rule applies to every robot you will ever make, from a toy car to a factory arm.

Common beginner mistakes

Almost every new builder makes the same small mistakes. Knowing them in advance saves you money, time and fingers.

Buying cheap tools that break

A very cheap screwdriver rounds off the screw head. A very cheap drill wanders off the mark. You then spend an hour fixing a problem a better tool would not have caused. Spend a little more on tools that touch a screw or a measurement.

Using the wrong glue

Beginners glue everything. Glue is fine for some jobs, but weak for others. Superglue is brittle and cracks under vibration. Hot glue peels off under load. For strong, lasting joins, use epoxy or, better, use a screw. A screw can also be undone when you need to fix something.

Mixing screw types and sizes

A drawer full of random screws is a trap. In the middle of a build, you grab a screw that almost fits. It damages the hole, and the thread strips. Standardise on one size, such as M3, and keep spares in a labelled bin.

Having no storage

Without storage, parts scatter. You lose time searching and lose parts you already paid for. Bins and labels cost almost nothing and pay back every single build.

Working with bad light and a messy bench

Poor light hides mistakes. A cluttered bench hides tools and causes accidents. Clear the build zone before you start. Good light and open space make careful work easy.

How professionals do it

When you move from a desk to a real product, the ideas stay the same. The scale, precision and planning grow. Here is what changes.

Design in CAD first

CAD stands for computer-aided design. It is software where you build a 3D model of every part. You check fits on the screen before you cut anything. A professional rarely cuts a part without a CAD model first.

Think about tolerance stacks

A tolerance stack is the sum of many small errors. Five parts, each slightly too big, can add up to a shaft that will not fit. Professionals add up the worst case before they build. If the stack fails, they loosen one tolerance or redesign the joint.

Source parts from datasheets

A datasheet is the official document for a part. It lists exact sizes, materials, loads and limits. Professionals choose parts from datasheets, not from a photo. The datasheet tells you the truth.

Use CNC and sheet-metal services

For final parts, professionals send drawings to a workshop. CNC means computer numerical control: a machine cuts metal from a digital file. Sheet-metal services cut and bend flat plates into strong shapes. These services give repeatable, accurate parts that hand tools cannot match.

Buy in a planned way

Professionals keep a bill of materials. A bill of materials, or BOM, is a list of every part, its size, its quantity and its supplier. Order from the BOM, and nothing is missing when the build day comes. You can keep a simple BOM in a notebook or a spreadsheet.

Note

You can borrow every professional habit today, for free. Make a parts list. Model a simple part in free CAD. Check sizes on the datasheet. These habits scale from your desk to a factory.

Try it yourself

Reading builds understanding. Doing builds skill. These five exercises go from easy to harder. Each takes under an hour.

Exercise 1 — Measure ten things

Pick ten small objects: a coin, a pen, a screw, a cable. Measure each one with your caliper. Write down every number. Hint: measure each object twice. If the two numbers differ, adjust your grip and try again.

Exercise 2 — Identify your screws

Collect the screws you own. Measure the diameter of each one. Sort them into M2, M3 and M4 piles. Count how many of each size you have. Hint: keep a paper label with each pile so you never mix them again.

Exercise 3 — Make a foam-board mock-up

Sketch a small two-wheel robot on paper. Cut the shape from foam board. Place your battery, board and motors on it. Move the parts until everything fits. Hint: leave space around each part for wires and for your fingers.

Exercise 4 — Build a fastener test joint

Take two scraps of plywood. Drill an M3 hole through both. Join them with a bolt, washer and nyloc nut. Then try the same joint with superglue, and pull both apart gently. Hint: the bolted joint survives. This shows why screws beat glue under load.

Exercise 5 — Set up and label your bench

Clear one table. Mark the four zones with tape. Put a bright lamp over the build zone. Add bins and label them by screw size and part type. Hint: do this before your next build, not during it. A tidy start makes every later step faster.

Going deeper: reading a tolerance stack (optional)

This part is optional. Skip it now and come back when you want precision. Here is a small tolerance stack you can try today.

Imagine three parts in a row: a base 20.0 mm, a spacer 5.0 mm and an end block 10.0 mm. Each part may be 0.1 mm too big or 0.1 mm too small. In the worst case, all three are too big, and the total grows by 0.3 mm.

Worst-case stack total = 20.0 + 5.0 + 10.0 = 35.0 mm ± 0.3 mm

The real total may be anywhere from 34.7 mm to 35.3 mm. If your fourth part must fit in exactly 35.0 mm, it will not fit in half of your builds. The fix is to leave a small gap, or to give one part a looser tolerance. Professionals do this maths before ordering parts. It is the quiet secret behind machines that always fit.

Key takeaways

  • You do not need many tools — you need the right ones for each job.
  • Buy a first wave of cheap, useful tools, then add power tools when a project needs them.
  • Match materials to the job: aluminium for light strong frames, wood and foam for cheap prototypes, polycarbonate for toughness.
  • M3 screws are the robotics standard because they are strong, small and sold everywhere.
  • Tolerances matter: 0.1 mm decides whether a shaft spins or jams.
  • Set up four bench zones, use bright light, wear safety glasses, and label your storage bins.
  • Prototype in cheap material, then build the final part in the real material.

Answer. A screw can be tightened and undone, and it survives vibration better than brittle glue. Glue cracks or peels under load, and it cannot be adjusted.
