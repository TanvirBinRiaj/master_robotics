Appendix A

Glossary of Robotics Terms

Every important word in this book, explained in simple English. Keep this page open while you read. If a chapter uses a word you forget, look it up here in seconds.

Reference All levels Appendix A of B

The glossary is grouped by topic so related ideas sit together. Within each group, terms are alphabetical. Where a word is explained deeply in a chapter, the chapter number is shown.

How to use this page

Press Ctrl + K (or Cmd + K on a Mac) and type the word to jump straight to its chapter. Or use your browser's search (Ctrl + F) to find a term on this page.

Core ideas

TermPlain-English meaning
RobotA machine that can sense its world, decide what to do, and act on that decision without a person controlling every move. (Ch. 1)
SenseTo measure something about the world, such as distance, light or touch. (Ch. 1)
DecideTo choose the next action, usually by following a program. (Ch. 1)
ActTo move or do something in the world, such as turning a wheel. (Ch. 1)
ActuatorA part that creates movement. Most actuators are motors. (Ch. 1, 6)
ControllerThe small computer that reads sensors and decides what to do. The robot's brain. (Ch. 1, 11)
SensorA part that measures the world and turns it into a signal. (Ch. 1, 10)
FeedbackUsing a measurement of what happened to adjust the next action. (Ch. 15)
Control loopThe repeating cycle of sense, decide, act. Also called the robot loop. (Ch. 1, 15)
AutonomyThe ability of a robot to work on its own, without a human giving each command. (Ch. 1, 20)
TeleoperationControlling a robot by hand from a distance. The opposite of autonomy. (Ch. 1)

Design and method

TermPlain-English meaning
RequirementA clear, measurable thing the design must do. "Run for 30 minutes" is a requirement. (Ch. 2)
SpecificationThe full written list of requirements and limits for a design. (Ch. 2)
ConstraintA limit you cannot break, such as maximum cost, weight or size. (Ch. 2)
Trade-offGiving up one good thing to gain another, such as lighter weight for higher cost. (Ch. 2, 8)
DecompositionBreaking a big problem into small, testable pieces. (Ch. 2)
PrototypeAn early, rough version built to test an idea quickly. (Ch. 2, 22)
ToleranceHow much a real part may differ from the ideal size and still work. (Ch. 4)
Design for manufacture (DFM)Designing parts so they are cheap and easy to make at scale. (Ch. 8)
Design for assembly (DFA)Designing a product so it is quick and easy to put together. (Ch. 8)
ServiceabilityHow easy a robot is to repair and maintain. (Ch. 8)

Mechanics and motion

TermPlain-English meaning
ChassisThe frame that holds all the parts of a robot in the right place. (Ch. 5)
Degrees of freedom (DoF)The number of independent ways a robot can move. (Ch. 5)
Revolute jointA joint that turns, like a door hinge. (Ch. 5)
Prismatic jointA joint that slides in a straight line, like a drawer. (Ch. 5)
Kinematic chainBodies joined one after another by joints, like the links of an arm. (Ch. 5, 16)
TorqueThe turning force of a motor. The strength with which it can twist something. (Ch. 6)
Stall torqueThe maximum twisting force a motor gives when it is held still and cannot turn. (Ch. 6)
RPMRevolutions per minute. How many full turns a motor makes in one minute. (Ch. 6)
Gear ratioHow many times a driving gear turns for one turn of the driven gear. It trades speed for torque. (Ch. 7)
BacklashThe small free play between gears before they push each other. (Ch. 7)
Lead screwA threaded rod that turns spinning motion into straight-line motion. (Ch. 7)
BearingA part that lets something spin smoothly with little friction. (Ch. 7)
Centre of massThe balance point of a robot, where its weight acts. (Ch. 8)
Base of supportThe area under a robot that touches the ground. A robot tips if its centre of mass leaves this area. (Ch. 8)
InertiaHow strongly an object resists a change in its motion. (Ch. 8)
StiffnessHow little a part bends when a force is applied. (Ch. 5, 8)
ComplianceDesigned flexibility, so a part gives way a little on purpose. (Ch. 5)
End effectorThe tool at the end of a robot arm, such as a gripper or a welding tip. (Ch. 21)
GripperThe end effector that holds objects. (Ch. 21)
PayloadThe weight a robot can carry or lift in addition to itself. (Ch. 21)

Electronics and power

TermPlain-English meaning
Voltage (V)The electrical "push" or pressure that moves charge. Measured in volts. (Ch. 9)
Current (I)The flow of electrical charge. Measured in amperes (amps). (Ch. 9)
Resistance (R)How strongly a part slows the flow of current. Measured in ohms. (Ch. 9)
ResistorA part that limits current, often to protect an LED. (Ch. 9)
CapacitorA part that stores a small amount of charge and smooths sudden changes. (Ch. 9)
DiodeA part that lets current flow in one direction only. (Ch. 9)
LEDLight-emitting diode. A small light that glows when current flows through it. (Ch. 9)
TransistorA tiny electronic switch, used to control large currents with a small signal. (Ch. 9)
H-bridgeA circuit that lets a motor spin forward or backward. (Ch. 6, 13)
Motor driverA board or chip that supplies the strong current a motor needs, controlled by the brain. (Ch. 6, 13)
GroundThe common return path for current. The "zero" reference point of a circuit. (Ch. 9)
Short circuitA wrong path where current flows with almost no resistance — dangerous and often damaging. (Ch. 9)
DCDirect current. Current that flows in one direction, as from a battery. (Ch. 9)
ACAlternating current. Current that reverses direction many times a second, as from a wall socket. (Ch. 9)
MultimeterA tool that measures voltage, current and resistance. (Ch. 9, 13)
EncoderA sensor inside or on a motor that reports how far or how fast it has turned. (Ch. 6, 10)
BreadboardA reusable board with holes for building circuits without soldering. (Ch. 4, 9)
SolderingJoining metal parts with melted metal to make a permanent electrical connection. (Ch. 13)
Battery capacity (Ah)How much charge a battery holds, in amp-hours. (Ch. 12)
Voltage regulatorA part that gives a steady output voltage even when the input changes. (Ch. 12)
Flyback diodeA diode that safely absorbs the voltage spike from a motor when it switches off. (Ch. 9, 13)

Sensing and perception

TermPlain-English meaning
SignalThe useful, changing value a sensor produces. (Ch. 10)
NoiseRandom, unwanted wobble added to a signal. (Ch. 10)
FilteringSmoothing a noisy signal to make it more useful. (Ch. 10, 15)
CalibrationAdjusting a sensor so its readings match the real world. (Ch. 10)
Analogue signalA signal that can take any value in a range, like a smooth dimmer. (Ch. 10)
Digital signalA signal with only two states, on or off. (Ch. 10)
Ultrasonic sensorA sensor that measures distance using sound too high for humans to hear. (Ch. 10)
LiDARA sensor that measures distance using laser light, often sweeping to build a map. (Ch. 10, 17)
IMUInertial measurement unit. A sensor that measures rotation and acceleration. (Ch. 10)
CameraA sensor that turns light into an image of numbers. (Ch. 17)
PixelOne tiny dot in an image, with colour values. (Ch. 17)
Depth sensingMeasuring how far away each part of a scene is. (Ch. 17)
FeatureA useful, recognisable pattern in an image, such as a corner or an edge. (Ch. 17)
Object detectionSoftware that finds and names objects in an image. (Ch. 17, 19)
Sensor fusionCombining several sensors to get a better answer than any one alone. (Ch. 10, 20)

Software and control

TermPlain-English meaning
MicrocontrollerA tiny computer that runs one program, reads sensors and drives actuators. (Ch. 11, 14)
Single-board computerA small full computer, often running Linux, for heavier tasks like vision. (Ch. 11)
FirmwareThe program stored on a microcontroller. (Ch. 14)
Loop rateHow many times per second the robot's control loop runs. (Ch. 14)
PWMPulse-width modulation. A way to control motor speed by switching power on and off very fast. (Ch. 14)
Open-loop controlActing without checking the result. Simple but inaccurate. (Ch. 15)
Closed-loop controlActing, measuring the result, and correcting. More accurate. (Ch. 15)
ErrorThe gap between what you want and what you have. (Ch. 15)
PID controllerA common control method that uses three terms — proportional, integral, derivative — to reduce error smoothly. (Ch. 15)
SetpointThe target value you want the robot to reach. (Ch. 15)
KinematicsThe study of motion without worrying about forces: where parts are and how they move. (Ch. 16)
Forward kinematicsWorking out where the end of an arm is, given the joint angles. (Ch. 16)
Inverse kinematicsWorking out the joint angles needed to reach a target position. (Ch. 16)
FrameA coordinate system attached to a part, used to describe positions. (Ch. 16)
ROSRobot Operating System. Not an operating system, but a large toolbox of software for building robots. (Ch. 18)
NodeIn ROS, one small program that does one job. (Ch. 18)
TopicIn ROS, a named channel where nodes publish or subscribe to data. (Ch. 18)
MessageIn ROS, the typed data sent on a topic. (Ch. 18)
ServiceIn ROS, a request-and-reply call between nodes. (Ch. 18)
ActionIn ROS, a long task that sends feedback while it runs. (Ch. 18)

Intelligence and navigation

TermPlain-English meaning
Artificial intelligence (AI)Software that can learn or make smart choices. (Ch. 1, 19)
Machine learningTeaching a program by showing it many examples instead of writing rules by hand. (Ch. 19)
TrainingThe learning stage, when the program studies examples and improves. (Ch. 19)
InferenceRunning a trained model to get an answer in real time. (Ch. 19)
Neural networkA type of learning program built from many simple connected units. (Ch. 19)
Reinforcement learningLearning by trying actions and getting rewards for good results. (Ch. 19)
MapA model of the robot's surroundings used for planning. (Ch. 20)
LocalisationWorking out where the robot is on the map. (Ch. 20)
SLAMBuilding a map and finding your place in it at the same time. (Ch. 20)
Path planningChoosing a route from here to a goal while avoiding obstacles. (Ch. 20)
Global plannerPlans the whole route using the map. (Ch. 20)
Local plannerHandles the next few metres, dodging things the map missed. (Ch. 20)
Obstacle avoidanceReacting to nearby objects so the robot does not hit them. (Ch. 20)
GraspA specific way of holding an object with a gripper. (Ch. 21)
Pick and placeThe common task of picking an object up and putting it somewhere else. (Ch. 21)

Safety and professional practice

TermPlain-English meaning
PPEPersonal protective equipment: safety glasses, gloves, closed shoes and so on. (Ch. 3)
E-stopEmergency stop. A big button that cuts power to the robot instantly. (Ch. 3)
Lockout / tagoutA safety rule: lock the power off and label it so no one can switch it on while you work. (Ch. 3)
Risk assessmentWriting down what could go wrong and how you will prevent it. (Ch. 3)
Risk / rewardWeighing the chance of harm against the benefit of an action. (Ch. 3)
PortfolioA collection of your real projects that shows what you can do. (Ch. 24)
IterationImproving a design by repeating the build-test-improve cycle. (Ch. 2, 24)
Version controlTracking changes to files over time so you can go back and work with others. (Ch. 2, 24)
Test planA written list of the checks a design must pass. (Ch. 2, 22)
Robot as a Service (RaaS)Paying to use a robot by the month instead of buying it. (Ch. 23)

Advanced foundations (Part 7)

TermPlain-English meaning
SO(3)The set of all 3D rotations. A formal name for "every way a body can turn". (Ch. 25)
SE(3)The set of all 3D poses: position plus rotation together. (Ch. 25)
QuaternionA four-number way to store a rotation. No gimbal lock, easy to blend. (Ch. 25)
Lie groupA smooth set of transforms, such as rotations, that can be combined and undone. (Ch. 25)
Convex optimization / QPFinding the best choice under limits, where the problem shape guarantees one best answer. QP means the cost is quadratic and limits are straight lines. (Ch. 25)
OSQP / HPIPM / qpOASESPopular QP solver programs robots use to solve optimization fast. (Ch. 25, 26)
MPCModel predictive control. Plan a short future with a model, run the first step, then replan. (Ch. 26)
Whole-body control (WBC)Controlling all joints at once so many tasks share one body without fighting. (Ch. 26)
NullspaceThe leftover freedom after a main task is done. Used for second tasks that must not disturb the first. (Ch. 26)
Impedance controlControlling how a robot pushes back, like a tunable spring, instead of only its position. (Ch. 30)
ros2_controlThe ROS 2 framework that connects controllers to real hardware joints. (Ch. 27)
EtherCAT / CANopen (CiA 402)Fast industrial networks and drive profiles that link controllers to motors in real time. (Ch. 27)
PREEMPT_RTA Linux mode that makes timing predictable enough for fast control loops. (Ch. 27)
System identificationMeasuring a real robot so the simulator matches it: mass, friction, motor strength. (Ch. 28)
Domain randomizationTraining in many random simulated worlds so the real world feels familiar. (Ch. 28)
Factor graphA web of measurement clues linking poses and landmarks, solved all at once for SLAM. (Ch. 25)
Performance Level (PL)The ISO 13849 safety grade of a safety function, from a (lowest) to e (highest). (Ch. 29)
SILSafety integrity level. The IEC 61508 grade of how well a system avoids dangerous failure. (Ch. 29)
FMEAFailure mode and effects analysis. Listing each way a part can fail and what happens next. (Ch. 29)
MoveIt 2The ROS 2 arm-planning toolbox: plans collision-free motions with planners like OMPL. (Ch. 30)
VLAVision-language-action model. A robot brain that turns what it sees and hears into actions. (Ch. 30)

Key takeaways

  • A word you can define in your own words is a word you own.
  • Most terms are simple ideas with a formal name attached.
  • Jump to the chapter beside a term to see it used in context.
  • Re-read a definition when you meet a word again; repetition builds fluency.
